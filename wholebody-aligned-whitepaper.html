Not for clinical or diagnostic use. WholeBody Aligned™ is a conceptual framework offered for dialogue and research. It is not a clinical, diagnostic, therapeutic, or assessment tool. Its concepts, including Alignment Tension and Interior Coherence Rupture, must not be used to diagnose, assess, label, or make decisions about any individual. Nothing in this paper is medical, psychological, or pastoral advice. Anyone in distress should seek help from a qualified professional.

Abstract

Human flourishing involves the wholeness of a life: the body, mind and heart, relationships, spiritual life, and daily conduct held together in meaningful relationship rather than experienced as disconnected dimensions. WholeBody Aligned™ proposes a framework for characterizing this wholeness through the relationships among five domains of human life.

The framework identifies five domains: Physiological, Mental-Emotional, Relational, Spiritual, and Behavioral. It proposes that these domains continually influence one another, so that strain in one may affect the condition of the whole. It calls the divergence among them Alignment Tension: the internal friction that may arise when different dimensions of a life pull in opposing directions.

The framework distinguishes coherence from flourishing. Coherence concerns the relational condition of the person. Flourishing is broader, encompassing meaning, dignity, purpose, relationship, and moral orientation.

This paper invites theologians, spiritual directors, chaplains, contemplative practitioners, and researchers into dialogue. It asks whether a more integrated account of human coherence can serve whole-person understanding without reducing the person to measurable variables. A few core relationships are stated symbolically in the Appendix; the framework and its proposed measurements remain subject to conceptual refinement and empirical validation.

1. Introduction: The Fragmented Person

Modern care divides the person into parts. The physician tends the body, the therapist the mind, the social worker the household, and the chaplain the soul. Each works within its own vocabulary, and rarely do they speak to one another.

This division has brought real gains, but it carries a cost. A grieving widow may present with high blood pressure, poor sleep, withdrawal from friends, and a quiet loss of faith. Treated in pieces, she may receive four separate responses and still not be met as one person.

Medicine also tends to define health as the absence of disease. It has little language for what health is for, or for the positive state the traditions have called peace, wholeness, or the good life. Engel's biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) named the need for integration but offered no way to describe how the parts interact.

The wisdom traditions have never accepted this fragmentation. They have spoken of the person as a unity of body and spirit, set within community and oriented toward meaning. WholeBody Aligned™ begins from that same conviction and asks whether it can be described with care and precision.

2. Central Thesis: Flourishing as Wholeness

Human flourishing emerges when the domains of a life are held together in mutual support. It cannot be built by perfecting one part in isolation. It arises from the harmony of the whole.

Three claims follow from this thesis:

  • The person is a unity. Body, mind, relationships, spirit, and conduct are not separate compartments. They form one living whole in constant exchange.
  • Strain travels. Disturbance in one domain is felt throughout. Chronic illness can darken the spirit; isolation can wear on the body; a loss of meaning can unravel daily habits.
  • Wholeness is more than the sum of parts. A life can excel in one area and still be divided. Flourishing depends on reducing the friction between domains, not only on strengthening each one.

On this view, flourishing is not a static achievement but a living equilibrium. It is sustained over time, renewed through daily practice, and tested in suffering.

Coherence and flourishing are related but distinct

Coherence is not, by itself, a complete definition of human flourishing. A person may show substantial consistency across beliefs, relationships, and behavior while pursuing destructive purposes. Conversely, a person in grief, doubt, illness, or moral struggle may retain profound dignity, purpose, and meaningful engagement with life.

WholeBody Aligned™ therefore treats coherence as a dimension of human wholeness, not a sufficient measure of human worth or flourishing. It seeks to characterize the relationships among the domains of a life. It does not claim that a numerical score can establish the meaning, goodness, or ultimate value of a person's existence.

What coherence is

Coherence is the degree to which the domains of a life support rather than undermine one another over time. It is a relational property: it describes how the parts of a life work together, not how strong any single part is.

Coherence reserve

Alongside coherence, the framework attends to coherence reserve: the capacity a person can draw on to absorb strain without losing integrated functioning. Reserves come from many sources, including physical health, emotional steadiness, supportive relationships, a living sense of meaning, and sustaining routines.

Coherence describes how well the domains work together now; reserve describes how much strain they can absorb before that working-together fails. A person can be coherent with thin reserves, and coherence can hold through severe hardship when reserves are deep. This distinction becomes central in Section 6, where the exhaustion of reserves marks the point of rupture.

3. The Five Domains of Coherence

WholeBody Aligned™ describes a human life through five domains, each held as an equal partner. None is reduced to another, and none stands above the rest.

Domains, constructs, and observables

The five domains are distinct dimensions of human life, not isolated systems or independent compartments. Each contains several constructs, the particular properties being characterized. Observables then provide evidence for estimating those constructs.

  • A domain identifies which aspect of human life is being considered.
  • A construct identifies the particular property being characterized.
  • An observable identifies the evidence used to estimate that property.

No single observable represents a whole domain. Heart rate variability is not physiological coherence, and a loneliness score is not relational coherence. Measurements are necessarily partial views of a more complex human reality.

Why these five

The five domains are not an arbitrary list. They follow from the basic ways a person exists: as a body; with an inner life of thought and feeling; among other people; oriented toward what they hold most meaningful or ultimate; and acting in the world. Each of these corresponds to one domain.

The set aims to meet three tests:

  • Distinguishable. Each domain can change while the others hold steady. A person can be physically healthy yet isolated, or spiritually grounded yet behaviorally chaotic. If two domains always moved together, they would be one.
  • Minimally sufficient. Removing any domain leaves a recognizable form of division unaccounted for. Without the Relational domain, isolation in an otherwise healthy life disappears from view; without the Behavioral domain, so does the gap between intention and action.
  • Collectively exhaustive. Commonly proposed additions fit within the five or belong elsewhere in the framework. Cognitive life lies within Mental-Emotional; moral life spans Spiritual and Behavioral; work and finances are circumstances that shape load and reserves; the natural and social environment enters as context and demand rather than as a domain of the person.

The set is offered as a working architecture. If research shows a form of division these five cannot capture, the framework should be revised.

Physiological Coherence

The body's capacity to remain steady and responsive under stress. It includes the balance of the nervous system, the rhythm of heart and breath, metabolic health, and freedom from chronic inflammation (Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017; Furman et al., 2019). It is the body as a resilient dwelling rather than a machine merely free of breakdown.

Mental-Emotional Coherence

The stability and flexibility of thought and feeling. It describes the ability to meet difficulty without rigidity or collapse, to name one's emotions with nuance, and to hold one's own thoughts with awareness (Kashdan & Rottenberg, 2010). The traditions might call it equanimity or a settled heart.

Relational Coherence

The security and depth of a person's bonds with others. It asks whether relationships offer safety, empathy, and mutual care, or whether they are a chronic source of strain. Research shows that human beings regulate one another (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010; Cacioppo & Cacioppo, 2018); we are made to carry burdens together.

Spiritual Coherence

The alignment between a person's life and what they hold most sacred or meaningful. It includes a sense of purpose, consistency between values and actions, and a felt connection to something greater than the self (Puchalski et al., 2014). The domain is self-referenced: it asks how well a person's life aligns with their own spiritual foundation, whatever that foundation is, religious or not. The framework never defines the content of that foundation, only the alignment between it and the life being lived. This keeps the domain open to people of every foundation without reducing spirituality to a fixed list of beliefs or practices.

Behavioral Alignment

The faithfulness of daily conduct to one's deeper intentions. It covers rhythms of sleep and rest, movement, nourishment, the use of technology, and ethical action. It is where the inner life meets the world, the place where intention becomes practice.

Behavioral Alignment holds a distinct place among the five. It is partly an output: conduct reflects the state of the body, mind, relationships, and spirit. It is also a driver: habits of sleep, movement, nourishment, and attention reshape physiology, mood, and relationships over time. And it is where one particular form of incoherence lives, the gap between what a person intends and what they actually do. Someone may hold sound values, enjoy secure relationships, and be in good health, yet live in routines that work against all three. That gap belongs to none of the other domains, which is why behavior is treated as a domain in its own right.

4. How the Domains Shape One Another

The five domains are linked in every direction. Each one draws on the others and gives back to them. A change anywhere ripples everywhere.

Consider how a crisis of meaning can spread. When a person loses their sense of purpose, their thoughts may grow anxious and unsettled. The body answers: stress hormones rise, sleep falters, and inflammation increases (McEwen, 1998; Furman et al., 2019). Fear narrows the capacity to connect, so they withdraw from others. Healthy routines erode. What began in one domain becomes a strain on the whole person.

The same pathways carry healing. A restored friendship can calm the body. A steady practice of rest and prayer or meditation can settle the mind. Renewed purpose can give a person strength to rebuild their habits. Because the domains are joined, care offered in one place can bring life to all of them.

This is why the framework treats the domains as equals. The spiritual life is not merely a product of brain chemistry, and the body is not merely a vessel for the soul. Each has its own reality, and each participates in the wholeness of the person.

These relationships are neither equal in strength nor uniform across people. Their influence varies with circumstances, personal history, available resources, and the demands of life. The lines in the diagram above show conceptual relationships, not empirically established causal pathways.

5. Alignment Tension: The Divided Self

Alignment Tension names the friction that arises when the domains of a life pull in different directions. It is greatest not when every domain is weak, but when they diverge sharply from one another.

Alignment Tension is not a measure of nonconformity, disagreement, or deviation from social expectations. Human growth often involves legitimate tension among competing values, responsibilities, and circumstances. Uncertainty, grief, moral struggle, or spiritual questioning do not in themselves indicate a loss of wholeness. The concern is sustained divergence that creates internal friction or keeps one dimension of life from supporting another.

A person may be physically strong yet spiritually adrift, or deeply prayerful yet trapped in exhausting relationships. Such gaps create inner strain. The framework holds that a severe collapse in any one domain weighs on the whole life far more than its share would suggest.

Many people know this experience: acting against what they most value, or feeling like several people rather than one. Human reflection across cultures and centuries has described it as being divided against oneself.

Alignment Tension offers a way to describe that ancient experience in systemic terms. It does not replace the language of the traditions. It may help those who accompany others, such as clinicians, chaplains, and spiritual directors, to notice where a life is divided and where care might begin.

Coherence is not conformity

A coherent life is not necessarily a conventional one. Alignment among the domains does not require conformity to social expectations, cultural norms, institutional standards, or outside definitions of wellness. People may make difficult choices, challenge accepted conventions, or disagree with their communities while retaining real internal integrity.

The framework is concerned with the integrity of relationships within a person's life, not with whether that life meets an externally prescribed standard. It should never be used to treat legitimate dissent, unconventional beliefs, or differences in religious and cultural expression as evidence of personal fragmentation.

What Alignment Tension is not

Because Alignment Tension is central to the framework, it helps to distinguish it from neighboring ideas.

Tension is not stress. Stress is load placed on a person from outside: work, illness, loss, conflict. Tension is internal divergence among the domains. The two often travel together, but they can come apart. Maria (Section 6) carries real stress with little tension, because her domains remain in step. David carries high tension with modest outside stress, because his domains pull against one another.

Tension is not suffering. Suffering is something a person experiences. Tension is a condition of the relationships within a life, and it may or may not be felt. A grieving person may suffer deeply while their life remains whole. Someone else may feel fine while their life is quietly divided, and notice the division only when it gives way.

Tension is not inconsistency. Every life holds momentary inconsistencies: a skipped habit, a sharp word, a passing doubt. Tension refers to sustained divergence, the kind that keeps domains from supporting one another over time.

How tension behaves. Sustained tension is costly. Holding divided parts of a life together draws on coherence reserve, so high tension slowly depletes the resources a person needs for hard times and raises vulnerability to rupture. Tension eases through realignment, bringing the domains back into mutual support, not by pushing every domain to maximum strength. Often the most important step is not strengthening what is already strong but attending to the domain that has been neglected.

6. Flourishing as Sustained Coherence

Flourishing unfolds across a life, not in a single moment. WholeBody Aligned™ proposes that sustained coherence among the five domains may contribute to the conditions that support flourishing. Flourishing itself cannot be reduced to a coherence index or a measure of reserves; it also encompasses meaning, dignity, moral purpose, relationship, and the capacity to stay engaged with life through adversity (VanderWeele, 2017).

Three elements matter most:

  • Overall alignment. How well the five domains support one another at any given time. The framework calls this the WholeBody Alignment Index.
  • Reserves. The physical, emotional, relational, and spiritual resources a person can draw on when life grows hard.
  • The weight of stress against capacity. Hardship is not the enemy of flourishing in itself. What matters is whether the load exceeds a person's present capacity to bear it.

This account leaves room for suffering. A person in grief or illness may still flourish if their life remains held together and their reserves are not exhausted. Hardship can even deepen a person's wholeness.

It also points toward care that is patient and long-term. Flourishing is cultivated slowly, through practices of rest, community, prayer or contemplation, and faithful daily habits.

When strain becomes rupture

The framework distinguishes strain from rupture. A person may live through prolonged stress, uncertainty, grief, or conflict while remaining coherent enough to function. During such periods coherence may be degraded, but the person's inner life remains structurally intact.

Interior Coherence Rupture is different. It is a threshold event that occurs when the demands of life exceed a person's available coherence reserve. It is not simply a worse point on a continuum but a crossing of a boundary, after which reserves can no longer sustain integrated functioning.

Rupture may show itself across all five domains: breakdown of physiological regulation, loss of emotional regulation or clear decision-making, relational breakdown, collapse of meaning or values alignment, and an inability to turn intention into action. These are consequences of rupture rather than the rupture itself. The formal definition appears in Appendix A.2.

Three illustrations

The following are composite illustrations, not accounts of real individuals.

Flourishing. Maria is a nurse in her fifties. Her health is not perfect, and she carries ordinary stresses at work and at home. But her habits support her body, her friendships are steady, and her work expresses what she values. When her mother falls ill, she is stretched hard, yet her reserves, her community, and her sense of purpose hold. Her domains are not all strong; they are in step, and they carry one another through a hard season.
High tension. David is a successful executive in excellent physical condition. His career thrives, but his marriage has grown distant, and his work increasingly conflicts with values he once held close. He sleeps poorly and drinks more than he intends. No single domain has collapsed, but they pull against each other: strength in some is bought at the expense of others. This divergence, more than any one weakness, is the source of his strain.
Rupture. Ana has carried caregiving, financial strain, and her own illness for two years, coping steadily throughout. Then a sudden loss arrives that her depleted reserves cannot absorb. Within weeks her sleep breaks down, she withdraws from friends, her sense of meaning collapses, and she cannot follow through on basic plans. This is not a further step down but a break. Her dignity and worth are untouched. What she needs is support that rebuilds her reserves and restores her domains one by one, and with that support, coherence can be rebuilt over time.

The limits of measurement

Measurement may help characterize certain conditions of a life, but it cannot determine that life's ultimate meaning, dignity, or worth. A person living with profound illness, grief, disability, or spiritual darkness, or passing through rupture, must never be regarded as having diminished human worth because a measurement shows reduced coherence or depleted reserves.

7. Diverse Spiritual Foundations

People ground their spiritual lives in many different foundations. Some draw on established religious traditions, each with its own understanding of the person, ultimate reality, and wholeness. Others find meaning, purpose, and connection outside any organized religion.

WholeBody Aligned™ does not interpret, compare, or reconcile these foundations, and it does not derive its concepts from any of them. Spiritual Coherence is defined so that people of any foundation can recognize their own experience in it, on their own terms.

The author's own thinking about coherence has been shaped by the theology of Julian of Norwich, whose vision of a creation held in love informs the wider UCMS research program. This is noted as a personal influence, not as a framework the reader is asked to share.

8. Toward an Integrative Coherence Science

We propose that the integrated human person deserves to be studied as a whole. WholeBody Aligned™ points toward a possible interdisciplinary research program, which might be called Integrative Coherence Science. It would bring together research on interconnected physiological systems (Bashan et al., 2012), mind and emotion, human relationships, behavior, and the spiritual dimensions of experience, to examine how these interact over time.

This remains a proposed research direction, not an established scientific discipline. Its conceptual foundations, measurement methods, and empirical validity require further development.

Such an approach would shift the guiding question of care. Instead of asking only which treatment removes a symptom, it would ask what helps a whole life hold together and flourish over time.

Its work would include:

  • Studying how the body's systems, the mind, relationships, and the spiritual life influence one another.
  • Developing ways to notice rising inner strain before it becomes crisis.
  • Designing simple daily practices that serve body, mind, relationships, and spirit at once.
  • Building bridges between the language of meaning and the language of biology, so that each can inform the other.

The last of these is where theological partners are most needed. Science can describe patterns, but it cannot by itself say what a human life is for. That question belongs to the traditions.

Research context

WholeBody Aligned™ is part of the broader research program of the Upstream Coherence Measurement Stratum (UCMS), which examines coherence as a property of relationships within complex systems. Its companion work, the Standard Coherence Fidelity Layer (SCFL), addresses relational coherence in institutional and infrastructural systems. WholeBody Aligned™ extends this interest to the human person.

These applications share a measurement philosophy, an interest in relational structure and coherence. They do not assume that people and infrastructure can be characterized with identical observables, operators, or validation methods. Each requires its own domain-specific measurement and empirical justification.

Research status and limitations

WholeBody Aligned™ is a proposed conceptual and measurement framework. It has not yet established empirical validity as a unified measurement instrument. Key open questions include:

  1. Construct validity: whether the proposed constructs adequately represent their domains.
  2. Measurement validity: whether available observables give reliable estimates of those constructs.
  3. Cross-cultural fit: whether the framework applies across cultural, religious, and philosophical settings without imposing inappropriate assumptions.
  4. Added value: whether coherence measures tell us anything beyond existing measures of health, well-being, and social functioning.
  5. Ethical use: how to keep measurements from becoming diagnostic labels, tools of conformity, or judgments about individual worth.

The framework establishes no clinical diagnostic criteria, treatment recommendations, or predictions of individual outcomes, and it must not be used to assess or label any person.

9. Questions for Theological Dialogue

This framework is offered for conversation, not as a finished system. We invite theological and contemplative readers to engage these questions:

  1. Can the spiritual life be described without being reduced? Is it fitting to speak of "spiritual coherence" at all, and what would be lost if it were treated only as a measurable variable?
  2. Gift and task. The traditions often describe wholeness as grace received, not only an achievement. How should a framework of alignment make room for gift?
  3. Suffering and flourishing. How might the traditions' understanding of redemptive suffering, lament, and the dark night of the soul refine what counts as flourishing?
  4. Equal partners or a center? This paper treats the five domains as equals. Many traditions place the spiritual at the center of the person. How should that tension be held?
  5. Community and the self. Does an individual-centered framework underplay the communal, covenantal, or cosmic dimensions of wholeness that the traditions emphasize?
  6. Practice. How might chaplains, spiritual directors, and congregations use these ideas well, and where would they be misused?

Responses, critiques, and proposals for collaboration are warmly welcome at measurement@coherencemanagement.org.

10. Conclusion

WholeBody Aligned™ proposes that human flourishing cannot be understood by examining the dimensions of a life in isolation. Physiological condition, mental and emotional life, relationships, spiritual orientation, and daily conduct participate in a larger human whole.

The framework identifies coherence as a property of the relationships among these domains. It names Alignment Tension as an expression of their divergence and Interior Coherence Rupture as the point where strain exceeds what a person's reserves can bear. It proposes that sustained coherence, available reserves, and the demands of life may shape the conditions that support flourishing.

These ideas have deep roots in human reflection. What WholeBody Aligned™ seeks to contribute is an exploratory way of describing the relationships among dimensions of a life, in terms that may support dialogue between scientific inquiry and long-standing understandings of human wholeness. It remains open to correction, empirical testing, and theological refinement. It does not claim that a person can be fully represented by measurement, nor that coherence alone defines the good life.

The paper began from a simple observation: a life can excel in one area and still be divided. Everything in the framework is an attempt to take that observation seriously.

Can we better understand the integrity of a human life by examining not only the condition of its individual dimensions, but also the relationships that hold them together?

Appendix: Core Relationships

Why formalize at all? The framework is conceptual, yet a few of its claims are structural, and prose can blur them. That tension comes from divergence rather than overall level, that rupture is a threshold rather than a slope, and that flourishing accumulates over time are all easy to state loosely and easy to misread. Symbolic notation states these relationships precisely and makes each one testable in principle. It does so without assigning a number to any person: the notation clarifies shape, not quantity.

This appendix states three basic relationships in symbolic form. Each formalizes a claim made in the main text, and each makes that claim testable in principle:

  • Alignment Tension (A.1) formalizes Section 5's claim that tension comes from divergence among the domains, not from their overall level. It predicts that two people with the same average condition but a different spread across domains will carry different degrees of tension.
  • Interior Coherence Rupture (A.2) formalizes Section 6's distinction between strain and rupture. It predicts a threshold: integrated functioning holds, even under heavy load, until load exceeds capacity, and then breaks rather than gradually declining.
  • Flourishing as sustained coherence (A.3) formalizes Section 6's claim that flourishing unfolds across a life, shaped by reserves and demands. It predicts that the same coherence, sustained with greater reserves or under lighter load, contributes more over time.

These are structural claims, not a scoring method. No weights, thresholds, or measurement procedures are implied, and testing them requires measurement methods that remain to be developed.

A.1 Alignment Tension

Let D1 through D5 denote the conditions of the five domains. Alignment Tension increases with the divergence among them:

AT = f( divergence(D1, D2, D3, D4, D5) ),   f increasing

Tension depends on how far the domains diverge from one another, not on their overall level. A life whose domains are all strained but in step may carry less tension than one where a single domain has collapsed while the others remain strong.

A.2 Interior Coherence Rupture

Let Ω be a person's available interior coherence reserve and Ωr the critical rupture threshold. Rupture occurs when:

Ω ≤ Ωr

Equivalently, the dimensionless Rupture Index compares perturbation load E with available capacity κ:

Λ = Eκ,   Λ > 1 ⇒ load exceeds capacity

Rupture is a phase transition, not continuous degradation. Low coherence may persist for long periods without rupture; rupture is the boundary crossing at which reserves can no longer sustain integrated functioning across the five domains.

Formal statement. Within WholeBody Aligned™, Interior Coherence Rupture is the threshold event that occurs when available interior coherence reserve falls below the minimum required to absorb present perturbation load (Ω ≤ Ωr), or equivalently when perturbation load exceeds available capacity (Λ > 1), resulting in the loss of stable internal organization and a transition into a degraded coherence state.

A.3 Flourishing as sustained coherence

Let C(t) be coherence across the five domains at time t, with reserves Ω(t), load E(t), and capacity κ(t). Flourishing is pictured as coherence sustained over a span of life T:

Ψ(T) = ∫0T C(τ) · Ω(τ) · ( 1 − E(τ)κ(τ) ) dτ

When load exceeds capacity, the final term turns negative. That crossing marks rupture (A.2), not merely a smaller amount of flourishing. This expression shows how the parts relate; it is not a measure of flourishing, and it says nothing about a person's worth.
