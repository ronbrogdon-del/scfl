UCMS · SCFL reconstruction · Flint, Michigan, 2014–2016

Flint water crisis: the layers and the seams between them

Seven layers, from the river to the people who decided how it was used. Each seam is a place where two layers had to stay in step. In Flint they came apart one after another, and the signals at each seam were visible months before the emergency was declared.

Layer of the system Seam between layers
LAYER 7

Governance and oversight

State-appointed emergency management, the city, the Michigan Department of Environmental Quality, and EPA Region 5.

Seam 6 · Residents to regulators

Warnings reached the decision makers and were not acted on.

  • Jun 2015An EPA staff memo flags missing corrosion control and high lead.
  • Oct 16, 2015Flint switches back to Detroit water.
  • Jan 2016State and federal emergency declarations.
What SCFL would read: whether reports from below and decisions from above still moved together, or decoupled.
LAYER 6

Community and residents

Households reporting discolored, foul-smelling water and rashes, and organizing their own testing.

Seam 5 · Health to community

People felt the effects before any official measure confirmed them.

  • 2014–2015Repeated resident complaints at public meetings.
  • Aug–Sep 2015Citizen sampling with Virginia Tech finds elevated lead across the city.
What SCFL would read: the gap between what residents reported and what official monitoring showed.
LAYER 5

Public health

Blood lead levels, bacterial illness and disease surveillance.

Seam 4 · Taps to health

Contaminated water reached people's bodies.

  • 2014–2015A Legionnaires’ disease outbreak in Genesee County, with at least 12 deaths.
  • Sep 2015Hurley Medical Center study shows children’s elevated blood lead rose after the switch.
What SCFL would read: whether health indicators stayed coupled to normal water quality, or began tracking the change in source.
LAYER 4

Household taps

Premise plumbing in homes, schools and businesses.

Seam 3 · Pipes to taps

Lead and iron released from pipes arrived at the tap.

  • Oct 2014General Motors stops using city water at its engine plant because it corrodes parts.
  • Feb 2015Very high lead found in a resident’s home, prompting EPA attention.
What SCFL would read: whether water leaving the plant and water arriving at taps still matched.
LAYER 3

Distribution pipes

Aging iron mains and lead service lines carrying water to the city.

Seam 2 · Treatment to pipes

No corrosion control was added, so the protective scale inside the pipes broke down.

  • Aug–Sep 2014Boil-water advisories after coliform bacteria detections.
  • Jan 2015Notice of violation for disinfection byproducts (TTHM).
What SCFL would read: whether chlorine dosing, residuals and water age still held their normal relationship across the network.
LAYER 2

Treatment plant

The Flint Water Treatment Plant, brought back into full-time use to treat river water.

Seam 1 · Source to treatment

The source changed and the treatment did not change to match it.

  • Apr 25, 2014Flint switches from Detroit’s treated Lake Huron water to the Flint River.
What SCFL would read: whether treatment settings tracked the new source’s chemistry, or stayed fixed while the water changed.
LAYER 1

Source water

The Flint River, more corrosive than the Lake Huron water it replaced.

Cross-layer seam · Governance to treatment

The decision seam that set the rest in motion

The source switch was an interim cost decision made under state emergency management, and corrosion control was not required at the start. This seam skips the layers in between: a choice at the top changed the physics at the bottom, and each seam above it then had to absorb the change.

About this map. It is drawn from the public record to show the structure of the system: its layers, its seams and the dated signals at each one. It does not show SCFL readings. The “What SCFL would read” lines describe the relationship measured at each seam, not results.

Sources: Flint Water Advisory Task Force, Final Report (March 2016); U.S. EPA Office of Inspector General, Report 18-P-0221 (2018); Hanna-Attisha et al., American Journal of Public Health (2016).
