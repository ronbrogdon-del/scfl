Six Questions at the Frontier of Coherence, Faith, Science, and Human Flourishing

An essay for study and reflection

Ronald L. Brogdon
Founder, Upstream Coherence Measurement Stratum (UCMS)
October 2026

Introduction: What If We Are Not Measuring Everything That Matters?

Modern science has developed extraordinary ways to measure the world. We can measure energy, biological activity, economic performance, physiological function, social behavior, and the operation of complex technological systems. We can observe outcomes with increasing precision and build models that help us understand how systems behave under different conditions.

Yet a fundamental question remains: Are we measuring all the properties that determine whether a system can maintain its organization, adapt to disturbance, and continue to function as a coherent whole?

A system may exhibit acceptable performance while its internal relationships deteriorate. A biological organism may retain measurable functions while losing important capacities for recovery. An institution may continue to meet its immediate obligations while the relationships on which its longer-term functioning depends become increasingly fragile. A civilization may accumulate knowledge and technology while becoming less capable of replenishing the conditions that sustain human flourishing.

These possibilities raise a question about the nature of measurement itself. Perhaps some of the most consequential properties of complex systems cannot be understood through measurements of individual components or outcomes alone. Perhaps the relationships among components, and the changing organization of those relationships, deserve measurement in their own right.

The Standard Coherence Fidelity Layer (SCFL), developed within the Upstream Coherence Measurement Stratum (UCMS), represents an attempt to investigate this possibility. Its central proposition is that coherence may be characterized as a relational property of systems, upstream of certain observable outcomes. This is a research proposition, not an established universal law. Its scientific significance depends on whether the proposed measurements can be operationalized, independently replicated, and shown to contribute information beyond existing approaches.

But the implications of the question extend beyond engineering or systems science. They touch biology, consciousness, human flourishing, civilizational continuity, and the relationship between faith and empirical inquiry.

Six questions emerge from this larger investigation. Each offers a different entry point into a potentially common research program.

Question One: Is Coherence a Measurable Property Across Different Kinds of Systems?

The first question is deceptively simple: Can coherence be measured in a way that retains a meaningful common interpretation across biological and non-biological systems?

We routinely use the same words to describe very different phenomena. We speak of coherent thought, coherent organizations, coherent signals, coherent physical systems, and coherent social relationships. Yet the shared vocabulary does not establish that these phenomena possess the same underlying property. In some fields, coherence has precise mathematical definitions; in others, it serves as a broader description of coordination, consistency, or meaningful organization.

The challenge is to determine whether a more general, measurable relational property can be identified beneath these domain-specific meanings.

Consider an electrical grid, a biological organism, and a human organization. Their components are different, their governing mechanisms differ, and their purposes cannot be assumed to be equivalent. Nevertheless, each depends on relationships among components. Those relationships constrain how the system behaves, how disturbances propagate, and whether coordinated functioning can be maintained.

Could some aspects of this relational organization be characterized through a common measurement framework without pretending that the underlying systems are identical?

This is the distinction between a common measurement architecture and a universal law. A measurement architecture might offer consistent procedures for characterizing relationships, while the meaning and significance of its outputs remain dependent on the domain. A stronger claim—that the same numerical quantity represents the same underlying property across biological, social, and technological systems—would require substantially more evidence.

SCFL proposes that coherence can be approached as a measurable property of relational organization. The important scientific task is to determine exactly what that means operationally. What observations enter the measurement? What relationships are represented? Which transformations preserve the result? What conditions make comparisons legitimate? Under what circumstances does the measurement cease to be meaningful?

These are not peripheral technical questions. They determine whether cross-domain coherence is a genuine research possibility or simply an attractive analogy.

A rigorous investigation would select several domains, define the measurement independently of the outcomes it is intended to explain, and test whether the resulting values behave consistently under controlled changes. Researchers would also compare the proposed measurement with established domain-specific measures. If a common structure exists, the investigation should reveal where it persists and where it breaks down.

A negative result would be informative. It might show that coherence is measurable only within particular classes of systems, or that different forms of coherence require distinct mathematical treatments. Such a result would narrow the claim but could strengthen the science.

The larger possibility is that coherence could become a bridge between disciplines: not a replacement for their existing knowledge, but a means of examining a structural property that their separate measurements may characterize differently.

The fundamental question is whether coherence is merely a word shared across disciplines, or whether some aspect of relational organization can be measured consistently enough to support meaningful scientific comparison.

Question Two: Can Coherence Measurement Reveal Something That Existing Measurements Miss?

Science often advances by improving the accuracy of existing measurements. But another kind of advance occurs when researchers discover that they have been measuring the wrong aspect of a problem, or only one part of it.

This raises a second question: Can a system retain apparently acceptable performance while losing relational properties that are important to its continued functioning or recovery?

Consider a complex technological system. Its output may remain within acceptable limits even as relationships among components become increasingly constrained. A biological system may continue to perform a particular function while losing some of its capacity to compensate for disturbance. An organization may meet its immediate targets while the trust, communication, and coordination necessary for future adaptation weaken.

In each example, conventional indicators might accurately describe the system's current performance. The question is whether they adequately characterize its underlying condition.

This distinction is important. The proposition is not that existing measurements are necessarily defective. Performance, health, stability, and resilience measures can be extremely informative. Rather, different measurements answer different questions. A measure of current output does not automatically characterize the organization that produces that output, just as a measure of present stability does not necessarily establish the capacity to recover from a future disturbance.

SCFL approaches this problem from an upstream perspective. Instead of beginning with the outcome that a system produces, it seeks to characterize aspects of the relational organization associated with that system's state. The intended research question is whether such characterization provides useful information that is not already contained in conventional indicators.

To test this proposition, investigators would need to compare SCFL measurements with established measures in the same systems and over the same periods. They would ask whether the coherence measurements distinguish states that conventional indicators treat as equivalent. They would then examine whether those distinctions correspond to independently observed differences in functioning, response to disturbance, or recovery.

This requires particular care about timing. If a coherence measurement changes before a conventional indicator changes, that finding might establish temporal precedence. It would not, by itself, establish predictive validity or causation. The result would need to be replicated, and the analysis would have to account for alternative explanations.

The investigation should also include cases in which coherence measurements fail to distinguish meaningful differences. Otherwise, researchers risk selecting only examples that support the framework.

If the measurements consistently reveal additional information, coherence could become a useful complement to existing approaches. If they merely reproduce what conventional measurements already show, their additional value may be limited. If their meaning varies unpredictably across settings, the proposed framework would need revision.

Each outcome would clarify the actual contribution.

This question has practical significance because complex systems are frequently evaluated through indicators that are easy to observe, quantify, or report. A framework capable of characterizing relational conditions independently of those indicators might reveal a dimension of system state that is otherwise difficult to examine.

But the value would lie not in calling that dimension upstream. It would lie in demonstrating that the measurement provides information that matters.

The central test is whether coherence measurement tells us something independently useful about a system that we could not adequately determine from its existing measurements alone.

Question Three: Can a Civilization Replenish Its Capacity to Flourish?

The first two questions concern measurement. The third expands the inquiry to its human and civilizational significance.

What determines whether a civilization can replenish the conditions necessary for its continued flourishing?

Civilizations are often evaluated through their accumulated resources, technological capabilities, economic production, institutional stability, and ability to defend themselves against external threats. These are important dimensions of collective life. Yet they do not necessarily establish whether a society can reproduce the conditions that make its continued functioning possible.

A civilization can possess considerable material wealth while experiencing deterioration in trust, social cooperation, institutional legitimacy, intergenerational responsibility, or the ability to cultivate human capabilities. Conversely, a society facing significant material constraints may retain relationships and practices that support mutual care, adaptation, and renewal.

The distinction is between possessing resources and maintaining the capacity to regenerate the conditions required to use those resources well.

Here, replenishment means more than replacing depleted physical assets. It refers to the capacity of a society to renew the human, relational, institutional, and cultural conditions on which its future functioning depends. These might include the development of capable new generations, the restoration of social trust, the transmission of knowledge, the maintenance of institutions, and the ability to repair relationships following periods of conflict or disruption.

This idea presents a difficult measurement problem. Civilizational flourishing is not a single variable, and no one measure should be expected to capture it in its entirety. Moreover, the relationship between coherence and flourishing cannot simply be assumed. Some highly coordinated societies may be oppressive; some internally cohesive groups may pursue destructive ends. Coordination is not synonymous with goodness, and persistence is not synonymous with flourishing.

The question therefore requires both empirical and normative distinctions. Researchers must specify what kinds of flourishing they intend to study, whose flourishing is being considered, and which outcomes would count as evidence of improvement or deterioration.

Within those boundaries, upstream coherence measurement might contribute to the investigation of relational conditions that support or impede renewal. Researchers could examine whether certain patterns of institutional, social, or intergenerational relationships are associated with a society's ability to recover from disturbance, preserve essential capabilities, or sustain human development.

The research could begin at a manageable scale. Rather than attempting to measure the coherence of an entire civilization, investigators might study a hospital system, a community, a regional infrastructure network, or an institution undergoing prolonged stress. They could identify observable relationships, examine changes over time, and test whether the proposed measurements add explanatory value.

Only after such work would broader claims about civilizational replenishment become defensible.

There is also a philosophical dimension. A society may survive while becoming less capable of supporting meaningful human lives. It may maintain its institutions while failing to replenish the relationships that make those institutions worthy of preservation. Survival, continuity, and flourishing are related, but they are not identical.

This distinction gives the question its importance. It asks whether the long-term condition of a civilization can be understood not only through what it produces or possesses, but also through its capacity to regenerate the conditions of human flourishing.

The fundamental question is whether we can identify and measure relational conditions that help explain a society's capacity to renew itself, rather than merely documenting whether it continues to exist.

Question Four: Can Measuring Interior Coherence Contribute to Human Flourishing?

If coherence is potentially measurable in complex systems, does the concept have practical value when the system under consideration is a human being?

This question brings the investigation into a more personal domain. Human flourishing encompasses physical functioning, psychological well-being, meaningful relationships, agency, purpose, and the capacity to participate in a life that a person has reason to value. These dimensions overlap, but none can be reduced without remainder to a single physiological or psychological indicator.

Nevertheless, some aspects of human functioning can be measured. Researchers use physiological signals, behavioral observations, validated psychological instruments, social measures, and longitudinal outcomes to investigate how people respond to stress, maintain functioning, and recover from difficult experiences.

The question is whether an upstream approach to coherence could contribute something useful to this existing body of research.

The term interior coherence would need a precise operational definition before it could function as a scientific construct. It might refer to a defined pattern of relationships among physiological processes, psychological measures, or other observable variables. Those possibilities should not be conflated. Physiological coordination is not automatically equivalent to psychological integration, and neither should be treated as a complete measure of a person's well-being.

A productive investigation would begin by identifying a specific population, a clearly defined measurement, and an outcome relevant to flourishing. For example, researchers might examine whether a proposed measure derived from physiological signals contributes information beyond established measures of stress or well-being. Alternatively, they might investigate whether changes in a defined set of relational measures correspond to independently assessed changes in functioning over time.

The essential test is incremental value. Does the proposed measurement improve understanding beyond what researchers already know? Does it distinguish relevant states? Is the result reproducible? Does it remain meaningful across different people and circumstances?

The investigation must also guard against the temptation to turn a complex human experience into a simplistic score. A person who is distressed may nevertheless possess profound relational integrity, purpose, and compassion. A person whose physiological measurements appear well coordinated may be experiencing serious psychological difficulties. No single coherence measure should be assumed to establish whether someone is flourishing.

This is especially important if the research eventually addresses populations experiencing trauma, addiction, or other forms of suffering. Those questions require appropriate research ethics, validated measures, and qualified collaborators. An exploratory measurement should not be presented as a diagnosis or a substitute for established care.

Still, the possibility is meaningful. If a carefully defined interior coherence measure proves useful, it might help researchers characterize aspects of human functioning that conventional measures do not adequately represent. It could potentially complement existing approaches to understanding stress, recovery, and well-being.

The philosophical significance is equally important. Human flourishing is not simply the absence of dysfunction. It concerns the conditions under which people can live, relate, develop, and recover. A measurement that contributes to understanding those conditions would have value even if it explained only one limited aspect of the larger phenomenon.

The central question is whether interior coherence can be operationalized in a scientifically defensible way and shown to contribute useful knowledge about human functioning or flourishing beyond established measures.

Question Five: Could Agape Be an Upstream Candidate Invariant?

This question reaches furthest into the relationship between faith, philosophy, and empirical research.

Agape, commonly understood in Christian thought as self-giving love directed toward the good of another, is not merely an emotional state. It concerns a way of relating to others that can involve care, sacrifice, fidelity, and a willingness to recognize another person's good as something worthy of concern in its own right.

Within a theological worldview, agape can have a significance that exceeds anything empirical measurement could establish. It may be understood as an expression of divine love, a moral orientation, or a central feature of the relationship between human beings and God.

But what happens when we ask whether agape might also motivate a scientific investigation into the relational conditions of human flourishing?

The phrase upstream candidate invariant is useful here, provided its meaning is carefully bounded.

An invariant, in scientific usage, is a property or relationship that remains unchanged under specified transformations or across specified conditions. To propose agape as a candidate invariant would therefore require more than suggesting that love is important everywhere. It would require identifying an empirically meaningful construct associated with agape and specifying what is hypothesized to remain consistent across different settings.

For example, researchers might investigate whether relational practices characterized by sustained care, respect for others' agency, and a willingness to bear costs for another person's well-being are associated with identifiable patterns of cooperation, trust, or recovery. They could ask whether similar relationships appear in families, care institutions, communities, or other settings.

Yet the operational definitions would matter enormously. Trust is not identical to love. Cooperation is not necessarily altruistic. Sacrifice can be generous, coerced, or destructive. A cohesive group can act with extraordinary solidarity toward its members while harming outsiders.

Consequently, no single favorable social outcome should be treated as a direct measurement of agape. Nor should researchers assume that an observed pattern of coherence establishes the presence of love.

A rigorous program would separate at least three levels of inquiry.

First, there is the theological proposition: what is agape, and what does it mean within a particular understanding of reality?

Second, there is the philosophical proposition: what features of human relationships are essential to self-giving concern for another's good?

Third, there is the empirical proposition: can particular features of those relationships be operationalized and shown to have reproducible associations with measurable outcomes?

The third question can be investigated scientifically without claiming to settle the first.

This separation is not a retreat from the larger question. It allows the empirical work to proceed without requiring researchers to agree on theological premises. It also protects the theological question from being reduced to whatever a particular measurement happens to capture.

SCFL could potentially provide a framework for investigating the relational organization associated with such practices. It might help characterize how relationships are structured, how they change under stress, or whether certain forms of relational organization are associated with particular outcomes. But it cannot establish, by itself, that agape is universal, that love is a metaphysical invariant, or that God is the source of coherence.

Those are distinct claims requiring different forms of argument and evidence.

There remains, however, a genuinely interesting possibility: that a theological intuition about the fundamental importance of self-giving love could generate precise empirical questions that would not otherwise have been asked. The research might then discover where the proposed relationships hold, where they fail, and where the empirical findings challenge the initial intuition.

Such an approach would permit faith to motivate inquiry without dictating its results.

The central question is whether the relational implications of agape can generate testable hypotheses about human flourishing and whether any resulting empirical regularities are robust enough to warrant broader claims about the nature of relational organization.

Question Six: Can Coherence Measurement Connect Different Measurements—or Even Contribute to Consciousness Research?

The final question concerns the architecture of knowledge itself.

Scientific disciplines develop specialized methods because different phenomena require different forms of observation and analysis. Neuroscience measures neural activity; psychology investigates behavior and experience; physiology characterizes bodily processes; social science examines relationships and institutions. These methods are not interchangeable, and their differences often reflect genuine differences in the phenomena being studied.

Yet complex questions frequently cross disciplinary boundaries. Human consciousness, for example, involves questions about neural processes, subjective experience, cognition, bodily functioning, and the relationship between an individual and the surrounding world. Human flourishing raises similarly interconnected questions about the individual, social relationships, institutions, and the conditions of a meaningful life.

Could coherence measurement help characterize relationships among such measurements without replacing the measurements themselves?

This is the idea of an upstream measurement layer.

Imagine that several research teams study different aspects of the same population or system. Each team has a valid measurement within its own discipline, but the relationships among those measurements remain incompletely characterized. A common relational framework might allow investigators to ask whether the pattern of relationships among the variables reveals information not apparent from any single measure.

The opportunity is not necessarily to create one universal score. It may instead be to identify relational properties that can be examined alongside established measurements, while retaining the distinctions that make each discipline scientifically meaningful.

Such a framework could potentially be useful at the interfaces between physiological and psychological measures, between individual and social measures, or between biological organization and observable behavior. Whether it actually provides value would have to be established separately in each setting.

The same question becomes especially intriguing in consciousness research.

Consciousness remains a difficult scientific and philosophical subject. Researchers investigate neural correlates of conscious experience, mechanisms of attention and awareness, information processing, and the relationship between subjective experience and physical processes. No single measurement has established a complete account of consciousness.

Could coherence measurement contribute to this inquiry?

One possibility is that a defined relational measurement could help characterize aspects of coordination among neural or physiological processes associated with particular states of consciousness. Investigators could test whether the measurement contributes information beyond existing methods and whether it reliably distinguishes states that have been independently characterized.

But several distinctions must be preserved. Neural coordination is not equivalent to consciousness. Information integration is not automatically subjective experience. A relationship between a coherence measure and a conscious state would not, by itself, establish that coherence causes consciousness or explains why experience exists at all.

A further possibility concerns the interfaces between scientific descriptions. Perhaps a relational framework could help compare aspects of physical, biological, and psychological organization without assuming that these descriptions collapse into one another. Such a contribution would be methodological rather than metaphysical: it would help researchers formulate better questions about how different observations relate.

This is where the idea of measurement across measurements becomes particularly important. SCFL would need to demonstrate that it can do more than attach a common label to heterogeneous data. It would need to show precisely what relational information it extracts, why that information is meaningful, and what it adds to the interpretations already supported by the underlying measurements.

If successful, the framework might offer a way to investigate properties that emerge at the interfaces between disciplines. If unsuccessful, it might reveal that some apparent commonalities are analogies rather than measurable invariants.

Either result would advance understanding.

The central question is whether an upstream relational measurement can connect otherwise distinct measurements in a way that yields new, independently testable knowledge—and whether that contribution can extend to carefully defined questions in consciousness research.

Conclusion: One Research Program, Six Different Tests

These six questions are related, but they should not be treated as six conclusions that follow automatically from one another.

The possibility of measuring coherence across different systems does not establish that the measurement adds information beyond existing methods. Additional information about system organization does not automatically establish that the measurement can explain civilizational renewal. Nor does a useful measure of relational organization establish a connection to agape, human consciousness, or the deepest questions of faith.

Each transition requires its own evidence.

The value of considering the questions together is that they reveal a potentially coherent research program. At its foundation lies a methodological question: can relational organization be characterized in a way that is measurable, reproducible, and useful? From there, researchers can investigate whether the measurement contributes to specific domains and whether the resulting evidence supports broader interpretations.

The sequence might be expressed as follows:

  1. Measurement Can relational coherence be operationalized?
  2. Incremental value Does it reveal information existing measures miss?
  3. System renewal Can it help characterize recoverability and replenishment?
  4. Human flourishing Does it contribute to understanding human functioning?
  5. Agape Can theological and philosophical intuitions generate testable hypotheses?
  6. Cross-disciplinary inquiry Can it connect measurements and inform consciousness research?

A conceptual sequence of research questions, not a claim that each stage has already been validated.

There is an important distinction between the research program and the possible worldview implications of its results. The program can be pursued by investigators who have very different philosophical commitments. A scientist need not accept a theological interpretation to test whether coherence is measurable. A theologian need not accept a universal scientific invariant to investigate whether particular forms of relational organization are associated with flourishing.

Indeed, this independence may be one of the program's strengths. The research can remain open to evidence while allowing the questions that motivate it to be intellectually ambitious.

For you, the founder of UCMS, the challenge is to keep these levels connected without allowing them to become confused. The larger vision gives the research its significance; the individual tests give it scientific discipline.

And perhaps the most important question is not whether all six propositions prove true. It is whether pursuing them produces knowledge that would otherwise remain undiscovered.

A measurement framework earns its place through what it enables others to see, test, and understand—not through the breadth of the questions it claims to address.

If SCFL can help researchers discover a meaningful relational property, demonstrate where it matters, and establish where its explanatory power ends, that would be a substantive contribution in its own right. The broader questions about flourishing, agape, consciousness, and civilization could then be pursued with a firmer foundation.

That is the opportunity: not to claim in advance that all these subjects share one underlying answer, but to develop a disciplined way of finding out where their relationships are real, measurable, and consequential.
